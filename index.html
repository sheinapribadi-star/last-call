      How the line was drawn

      In 2023, Carissa Gardiner and colleagues pooled 24 studies of caffeine and sleep1. On average caffeine cost 45 minutes of sleep, added 9 minutes to falling asleep and cut deep sleep by 11 minutes. Their meta-regression named a cut-off for two drinks: a cup of coffee (107 mg) at least 8.8 hours before bed and a pre-workout scoop (217.5 mg) at least 13.2 hours before bed.

      Two cut-offs are not a rule you can use for a 16 oz drip and a 4 PM cola. So I asked a simpler question: is there one amount left in your body at lights out that both cut-offs agree on?

      There is. Caffeine peaks in your blood within an hour or two, then your liver clears a constant share of it every hour3. If it halves every 4.3 hours, the coffee and the pre-workout both leave 27 mg on board at bedtime. 4.3 hours sits inside the published adult range and a little under the 5 hour average. That 27 mg is the line, and the app keeps you under it from lights out until morning, however many drinks you stack.

      Self-check against the paper
      DrinkThe paperThis app

      Computed live at the average 4.3 h half-life. The one disagreement is black tea: the paper only names a cut-off once the 95% confidence interval for lost sleep clears zero, which a small dose may never do. I kept the stricter reading.

      Drake and colleagues saw the same thing in the lab: 400 mg taken six hours before bed still took more than an hour off objectively measured sleep2.

      What this is not. Not medical advice, and not a dose limit. It ignores body weight, genetics and tolerance, and pregnancy slows clearance far more than the slider allows. If you’re pregnant or on medication that interacts with caffeine, ask your clinician.
